=== As We Leave #82: The Pratap Files:13 Reasons why I love IITK ?? ===
Source: https://voxiitk.com/as-we-leave-82-the-pratap-files13-reasons-why-i-love-iitk/

In this 82nd edition of As We Leave 2026, Chinmay Pratap, a Y21 from the Department of Mechanical Engineering, reflects on an unforgettable journey defined by lifelong friendships, countless campus adventures, and the people who made every semester memorable. Through stories of laughter, traditions, and moments that turned into cherished memories, he celebrates the experiences that made IIT Kanpur much more than just a college, it became a place he will always carry with him.

“You can’t change the people around you but you can change the people around you”

This feels like a quiet blessing. Here I am, writing these pages in the scorching Kanpur heat, something I never imagined doing. The title might confuse some at first. I’ve spent exactly 13 semesters here 10+3 summer terms. It’s just a name for now or may be not, We’ll figure out the rest as we go. If you’re reading this, there’s a good chance it’s mostly me from the future. Maybe, just maybe, it reaches an old batchmate or someone who knew me back then or somehow a friend who I had a close friendship with. Either way, this is my journal. Some parts may not make sense if we weren’t that close. But I want to tell you that if we crossed paths and you come to this site to read “my” AWL: you have been special to me literally.

I still remember getting that call of Parteek while I was in Delhi. Everything felt electric, like a dream unfolding in real time. The excitement was real.
Later that night came the actual virtual meeting. I got introduced to the whole bhai culture and the warm, chaotic bappu vibe that defines this place. My school friend was already here, which made things easier. My first real connections were with these brothers one of them even from my own city.
It all started right there, simple and full of possibilities.

Sem 1 (2021-2022 I)

The start was a pure chaos, but in the best way. At that time I had just one close person I could talk to. We used to have five or six calls a day, sharing doubts, answers, and all the confusion. Then the online orientation kicked in. Everything felt brand new and exciting. I remember the anxiety before Wednesday TA labs and the Thursday PE quiz. Random groups popped up everywhere, Instagram connections bloomed, and memes kept flowing. After the long depressing days at home during COVID, this was a fresh breeze.We played online games like Smashkarts and Scribble to pass the time. I was terrible at them, while my Jaipur buddy (Georgebush) was a champion he used to dominate our whole wing and even Baap wing (*Subtle foreshadowing *). One highlight was our first offline meet in Jaipur with local buddies. We gathered at Patrika Gate. It was the first time turning online friends into real ones. That energy still feels fresh and online bulla’s were the best. Then exams  hit reality. I even don’t know what a quiz was. It sounds very casual and that is one reason we will be not taking about acads here (RR due to mechanical blood). Another highlight was having the FIR on us in TA 101 legit it was so weird. “Bhai, institute walon ne aate hi report card dene ke bajaye hum par report darj karwa di! Like wtf.” At the time it was stressful, but it became a lifelong memory. A senior bappu, active in gymkhana, gave us solid advice that helped calm things. I even performed stand-up comedy online as part of Humour House. The whole experience was overwhelming, in a good way  so much to absorb.

Welcome to IITK and stepping into the Hall 13 E511 and meeting the family of IITK was a cherished memory of summers of 2022 April. The kanpur city was never kind but just after crossing the railway gates it was clear that this is different and seeing all those beautiful visuals campus beauty and orientation with new wingies and friends and especially meeting our wing baaps and baaps and bhai’s. Hall 13 mess and living a life we always dreamt of as an IITian. The summer torture on 5 th floor was always one thing that I never wanted in my life. But making new friends wing bulla and new tradition of whole night cycling with wingie gang and chilling in his room after exams over is the thing that remained constant upto 8 sem.

After my already damaged academics, we stepped into the most talked-about semester for Y21 Mech batch. This was when we truly understood why it’s called “mechanical.” During attendance literally forgot the name of his course in front of Prof. The best part was finally connecting with solid people from the department. Many of them are still close today. TA201 played a big role it gave me some of the best memories and friendships.

Sem 4 (2022-2023 II)
Returning to campus meant facing the well known Kanpur winters. That semester tested me like never before. I was at an all-time low, and my resilience started cracking for the first time.

One afternoon stands out. I was exhausted and went to my bhai for the 12 noon class. He said, “Bhai, roz jaate hain… aaj chhodde te h, kya hi ho jayega ek din se.” I was already off that day, so I agreed. We skipped that one class : the only one I missed all semester and of course, the universe planned a surprise quiz that exact day (which was never in FCH btw). That course never went easy on us.

The highlight of the semester was joining the Election Commission (EC). It holds a very special place in my heart. Sem 1* (2022-2023 III)
Welcome to another episode of my f**ked up life. But if you’re still reading this, let me ask  how unemployed are you exactly? I’m 100% sure we’re solid friends if you’ve reached here. Let’s continue. That summer turned out to be one of the best times on campus. We temporarily shifted to Hall 12, and for a rare stretch, I actually felt some real hope. I was repeating course to clear my backlog, and most of the mechanical gang was with us (thanks IS). Mornings were simple and beautiful. I’d rush to the swimming pool with the wingie, enjoy the water  one activity I truly loved  then come back for breakfast, head to the library, attend classes, and actually stay productive. Evenings often meant going to DOAA with the same guy who had fractured his leg earlier. Nights were pure chaos and fun  late bulla sessions with new people from the branch, speedcubing buddies, or EC guys discussing campus politics.It was a much-needed breather, full of small joys and fresh energy.

Back to our den Hall 5, I got room A101. This was easily one of the best phases. Having my closest people nearby made everything special. I truly enjoyed some of my finest moments with the most supportive gang from IITK. This semester marked the peak bonding with the wingies. The Mech wingies especially stepped up and became the real protagonists of the story. We started studying in small groups of 3-4, which gave me the exact support and strength I needed. Evenings were pure fun  playing Smashkarts and Among Us for crazy stretches, including one 14-hour Sunday session. The Mech faculty also seemed a bit more empathetic this time. Nights meant frequent canteen strolls. My bruh and I got completely addicted to Chicken Kolhapuri and the mess’s kesar milk. It was one of the harshest winters yet, but we enjoyed it fully with extended wingies  making new friends and building stronger bonds ,crazy fest nights. Random 2 am rushes to the canteen for packed food with Georgebush became routine.  This was the semester I finally found people who gave me a new life. Group studies, late-night gaming, tutoring sessions with Arindom, and some important life-changing decisions everything started feeling worth it.

This was a whole new time when we started and making efforts of becoming what we always dreamt but somehow I have a very faint memories of this sem I remember most of the mornings I would be woken up by mech wingie scream scolding then rushing to class either to attend or to mark the attendance and then coming again to sleep and cycle repeats. That summer was one of the harshest. The heat was brutal  just surviving a single day felt like a challenge. The hall was literally burning. I was on campus doing a few extra courses while trying to figure out my next steps. I remember visiting some seniors who were about to leave, especially the Bappu wing and our mech godfather Vinoba. I went to them seeking advice about the Dual Degree program. I left the conversation still unsure. It was a quiet, uncertain phase. More than anything, I was just trying to survive.

Even if you woke me up years from now and asked for my academic peak sem, this semester would be my instant answer. I shifted to Hall 9 as final year began. Some people had moved to other wings, but the core mech wingies and a few solid ones stayed together. We also got some new, (and annoying) neighbours. This was the semester where everything clicked. Sem 8 (2024-2025 II)
And just like that, we’d reached the final sem of B.Tech. How time flies, na. Same for me. Every emotion this sem felt exaggerated, turned all the way up. I hate admitting it, but this was when I felt most connected ironically, right when everyone was about to scatter and finally start their actual lives. In Hall 9 everybody was loose, relaxed, most of them placed and coasting through a chill sem. And then there was me and some others. Sixty-four credits. A Mech overdose. Dragging myself to morning class from Hall 9 was a real struggle. I haunted every lab the Mech department had to offer due to some random course. SL canteen in afternoon with sarkar ki sena .The long night walks with the thala crew were peak, though. But I also kept myself a little isolated. I knew everyone would leave eventually, so to cope, I started pulling back, picking up other things to prep myself for the Masters chapter ahead. Somewhere in the middle of all this came Sikkim. A crazy trip, the best week of my life. We invented our own lingo out there  words that are still alive. Ask any of us what counts as batting and bowling, and you’ll get the whole dictionary. Back on campus, the photoshoot phase began with wingmates, branch buddies, everyone. Pure enjoyment. (Somewhere in there a guy fell flat on his head on the library foyer  genuinely a near-death experience for him.) The department farewell was the craziest night of all. And the small ritual that defined the sem: filling testimonials and getting them back for the yearbook. The most alive sem I’d had. After endsems it only got wilder. Night-outs, movies, cricket, swimming everything. Walking to the canteen at odd hours, just strolling, no plans. And then the goodbyes started. One by one, people began leaving. I was one of the last men standing. “As we move on!!” kept echoing around. It was the kind of time you could just cry over  and somehow, that’s exactly what I love about how it is.

Sem 3 (2024-2025 III)
New beginning, new chapter, new season, new cast  some old characters returning, some new ones joining. I came back hopeful, walking into the our den again Hall 5. New lab work, new lab members, new wingmates. The people who’d been recurring faces earlier were now main cast. And just like that, we were back in the game.The late-night walks became almost a daily ritual long loops out to DJAC, the helicopter building, all of it. A good stretch of time, really: adjusting, adapting, fighting to settle in. Walking all the way to Hall 3 for food was genuinely awful. But the people around me made up for it. They were great.

Sem 9 (2025-2026 I)
The new sem came with its own challenges courses, thesis, lab work, and nights spent at the lab and nowhere else. A new thing for me this time was TA duty, paired with a very good buddy and a “figure it out” kind of prof. The freshers were enthusiastic but annoying in equal measure  they gave us a lot of laughs through the term.

This was also the placement sem. The support I got was unreal not just from the people around me, but from a few old faces too. My thala group literally built my resume an hour before the deadline. And the seniors  especially Parv and Vinoba carried me through a genuinely tough phase. They helped, they showed up, they kept me going. Thankyou to each one of you.

Sem 10 (2025-2026 II)
The new sem began with a buddy leaving campus. That one sat heavy on me. A conference in Khajuraho came as a chill, relaxing break. Around this time a few good people to share meals with  one of them a total cracked maniac, but his easy vibe could lift a whole room’s mood. Another was disciplined enough to pull me into evening walks; that habit carried a big piece of me. After that, most days just rolled on. I shared lab free time with a buddy from the room next door  proper friends, both of us foodies. We strolled every food spot on campus, hitting Divyam almost every evening and Mama Mio late at night. I loved that whole stretch. We went on outings regularly and somehow just managed to stay happy, to enjoy this one fully. The long night walks with that buddy  talking about life in general were the constant through all of itThen came the thesis submission and defence. And right after, the string of goodbyes started again. Only this time we were mature about it. The last few days played out like an end-credits scene. Three or four of us stuffed into one room again, sleeping into the morning, heading to lab, eating far too much, hitting every campus food spot one more time trying to relive it all, one last time.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #82: The Pratap Files:13 Reasons why I love IITK]
And then, on 5 June 2026, at midnight, while the whole world slept, I crossed the campus boundary. Heavy heart, teary eyes. Looking back now, it all makes sense  leaving the place that gave me my purpose, my people, and everything I ever needed.

In the end, I want to thank everyone who’s been with me and IIT Kanpur itself, for giving me a life within me.
Netflix was a part of me throughout all of this, so let me end the way it would. The truth is, for so long I’d been stuck in one place  a deep, dark cave. Then I came here. And for the first time in a long while, I started feeling again. The feelings came alive in me.
Now, as we move on, there’s a bit of anxiety and a bit of excitement sitting side by side. But I hope everyone makes the best of their time, stays a good person, and that we all stay connected.

=== As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to Join IIT ===
Source: https://voxiitk.com/as-we-leave-83-a-letter-to-the-17-y-o-who-never-wanted-to-join-iit/
Date: 2026-08-11

In the 83rd edition of As We Leave 2026, Rashi, a Y22 student in the Department of Physics looks back on a four-year journey of exploration, setbacks, and finding her way. From navigating academics and discovering her interests to internships, research, higher studies, and friendships that became family, her story reflects how the paths we take and leave behind, shape who we become. As she leaves IIT Kanpur, she reminds us to cherish not just the milestones, but the quiet moments and people that made the journey worthwhile.
One of the things I’ve loved about IIT Kanpur is that this place makes you dream big. You enter
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
the gates of IIT Kanpur, a starry-eyed 17 year old, and leave 4 years later, with even bigger
dreams, people to call your own, and a story to tell. I don’t think it’s the same story your
first-year self would have imagined. In many ways, it makes you proud, in some, it’s tinged with
regret, but it’s a story to tell, laugh about, and remember regardless.
My story here began unexpectedly, and by that I mean it was unexpected for me too. I wasn’t
preparing to leave for college in 2022. I’d foolishly and rebelliously told my parents 2 years
before that, that IIT wasn’t for me because I didn’t want a corporate job because that was the
impression of IITs I had back then. So I decided to give JEE, about 7-8 months before Advanced
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
and although it was colossally stupid to start so late, I’m glad I started at all. After an intense few
months of hard work, questioning of past choices, and realising I hate studying Chemistry, I
found myself amidst these brick-toned walls.
1st and 2nd year:
I guess for most of us, first year is the most relaxed time here, without a wisp of the worries that
inevitably come later. It was especially relaxed for me because I didn’t concern myself with
anything, including academics. This of course reflected on my transcripts and led to (yet again)
much-needed questioning of my life choices.
Frankly, I didn’t expect people to take first year seriously at all, because most around me
weren’t. It was also a year of frequent HC visits and I felt out of place being surrounded by
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
people who had prepared for 2-3 years and seemed to have way more background practice
than me.
If there’s one thing I did right in my first year however, it was friendship. Through our shared
timetables during the day, and cycling and walks around campus at night, I made memories with
Ritik, Shreya and Shruti that I will always look back at fondly.
I had joined PHY because I enjoyed solving physics problems, but I did want to explore other
opportunities this place had to offer. In all honesty, my time here has been spent figuring out
what wasn’t for me, long before I figured out what was. I tried music, which although I still love, I
realised soon the MClub wasn’t a place I’d be happy in.
In my second year, I joined Motorsports and ICG. After a short time, I left Motorsports because it
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
was too time-consuming for me back then. After my secretary tenure at ICG, I realised that
although this data science and consulting work was meaningful, it was not something I enjoyed
much to continue in the future. In hindsight, I do wish I’d joined another SnT club or team which
I liked and would have continued with, like Aerial Robotics because I got into that but had picked Motorsports over it. At the same time, there’s a relief in knowing I did explore these things in my
time here.
By the start of my 4th semester I realised I should buckle up and took 65 credits (!?), leading to
a hectic but rewarding semester. In the summer, I found myself torn between wanting to prepare
for the internship season or preparing for higher studies. I decided to prepare for the internship
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
season, hoping that more exposure to these fields will help guide my decision later on. It was a
happening summer because I also took on some coursework I wanted to explore for my
research interest direction for the future, and a project. I realised I enjoyed DSA but not enough
for a full-time job, but prob stats was a lot more fun.
The thing about 2nd year summer is that it’s easy to take multiple things on your plate and get a
little lost. It also flies by really soon and before I knew it, Day 1 rolled around. I’ll always
remember the thrill of that morning and video-calling my parents right outside the tutorial block
telling them I got an offer from JP Morgan Chase.
3rd and 4th Year:
In my 3rd year, I delved more into Physics coursework and projects. Realising I definitely didn’t
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
want to go down the conventional corporate route, I started looking for research internships for
the coming summer. The process was long and mildly soul-crushing, but I soon ended up in
Beijing for a research internship at Tsinghua University.
The image my 2nd year-self had of how my 4th year would go, was very different to what
actually happened. It became the most hectic year of my time at IIT Kanpur, with intense
courses, projectwork and also applications for grad school. I was used to being dept. mentor
since 3rd year, but in 4th year I also took on the role of placement coordinator for the
department. I didn’t want the mistakes of my past to have a significant say on the grad school I
got into, and so that was a lot of added pressure for my applications. This was a strange time,
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
and I definitely had an unhealthy number of days when doing anything at all felt terrible. As a
cherry on top of this cake, I had a medical diagnosis at the end of my 7th semester (on the day
of a lab exam RIP) and had to undergo surgery soon after.
While looking back at my first-year self and earlier, I used to often be filled with anger and
regret. Over time, though, I’ve come to appreciate that many of those mistakes played a
significant role in shaping who I am today. I guess we grow and become who we are through
both our successes and failures.
Despite everything else and the unholy number of essays I had to write to convince universities
to let me in, I remember March 2026 as one of the most memorable months I’ve spent at IIT
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > As We Leave #83: A Letter to the 17 y/o Who Never Wanted to ]
Kanpur. Between all the official and unofficial batch video shoots, the campus seemed to be
running high in nostalgia and feel-good vibes. Shruti made it a point to announce (every day)
the number of days we had left on campus, and everything started feeling more special, with the
what-if of it being a potential “last”.
Those days also made me look back on how my friendships had grown through the years. From
walking around campus till sunrise, participating in competitions together, and attempts at
cooking pasta and pancakes in our hostel rooms, to becoming practically like family who would

=== The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup ===
Source: https://voxiitk.com/the-not-so-annual-health-checkup/
Date: 2026-09-22

On 29th July, 2026, a mail was sent out to all students of IITK about the initiative of a mandatory annual health checkup being conducted under directions from the Ministry of Education. The mail stated that this checkup is “conducted once every academic year”, which could misdirect one to think it had been in place for some time. However, this was the first time that such a health checkup was being conducted at IITK, and at such a large scale.
The mail mentioned that the checkup would begin in 2 days, i.e. 1st August, and would be conducted hall-wise. The first drive has since been conducted across most student halls. A second drive has also been planned in each hall for students who may have missed or avoided the tests earlier.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
In general,  a health checkup sort of sounds like a routine exercise involving, say, a blood test, basic health parameters and a general assessment of wellbeing. However, this initiative at IITK consists of two distinct components: a mandatory general pathology check for students across batches, and a separate, voluntary substance-use screening currently being conducted for the incoming Y26 batch.
With conversations around substance use and the ongoing Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan by the Government of India, the two exercises raise questions that go beyond the mechanics of the tests themselves. What was the need for such a checkup? Why has the government chosen institution-wide screening as a mechanism for student wellbeing? And once a student is identified as having a potential health or substance-use concern, what happens next?

[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
The mandatory health checkup

When asked about the specific purpose and rationale behind introducing a new health-screening drive for senior batches, the Dean of Students’ Affairs (DoSA), Prof. Pratik Sen, explained that the initiative stems from the National Education Policy (NEP) 2020.
Following a directive issued by the Ministry of Education in December 2025 for implementation from the 2026 academic year, institutes are required to conduct routine pathological tests for students.While the directive provides for mandatory testing, individual institutes have been given autonomy to determine the specific tests to be conducted, with the option of seeking expert guidance from either AIIMS Delhi or the National Institute of Social Defence (NISD).
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
At IIT Kanpur, the Health Centre (HC) finalised the tests in consultation with AIIMS Delhi. DOSA clarified that the Ministry of Education had issued two distinct letters: one for a voluntary drug test and another concerning a  mandatory pathology check.
Consequently, the administration of IITK conducted an optional drug screening exclusively for the incoming Y26 batch, both UG and PG, alongside a mandatory pathology drive covering all batches — Y26, Y25, Y24, Y23 and earlier.
The mandated pathology assessment consists of a blood sample, urine sample and questionnaire, along with standard physiological measurements. The blood tests include haemoglobin, blood sugar, SGPT and serum creatinine, while the routine urine examination (R/M) includes parameters such as colour, pH, protein and glucose. Standard vitals including blood pressure, body temperature, pulse, oxygen saturation, height and weight are also recorded.

[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
What happens to the results?

Addressing concerns regarding student privacy, DOSA assured that the administration would not have access to individual test results and that the data would remain strictly confidential and accessible only to the Health Centre. The medical staff would screen the test data to identify any underlying illnesses or health concerns. In case a medical issue is detected, the HC would reach out directly to the affected student to offer appropriate treatment.
Students, however, retain the choice of whether to pursue the treatment or counselling recommended by CMHW. They can decline or opt out of the process if they do not wish to continue.
However, there remains a question about access to the results themselves.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
At the time of reporting, the results of the pathology test  as well as the drug screening have not yet been made available to the students themselves. When initially asked about the reasons for this, DOSA said that he would look into the matter and would try to at least provide the results of the pathology test, although he could not promise the same for the drug test. He added that details regarding the drug test could be sought from the CMHW head.
After undergoing the process and providing their blood and urine samples, why are students not being given access to the results of their own tests? The same information is being used by the Health Centre to identify potential medical concerns and determine whether follow-up is necessary, but the student who provided the sample has not yet been given the opportunity to see those results.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
A few students have already begun receiving emails from CMHW regarding counselling, apparently based on their scores on the questionnaire. So even if it is intended for the results of these tests to be provided later, why does the HC or CMHW have access to them before students themselves?
To facilitate the large-scale testing, the Health Centre organised a hall-wise setup. The primary drive was conducted over the course of nearly a week. To accommodate students who missed their initial slots, the HC arranged a follow-up screening setup a few weeks later to ensure maximum coverage.
The mandatory nature of this test should also be under scrutiny. Even if the test is done with the best intentions and only intends to assist  & identify any potential healthcare concerns, why has it been made compulsory for students? What about the students who are genuinely uncomfortable with the process, given its invasive nature of requiring blood & urine samples?
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
Some students have also expressed concerns about the consequences of not participating. Students from certain halls, including Hall 2 and Hall 5, have reportedly received emails stating that failure to complete the mandatory test, without providing a valid and duly approved reason, would result in a ₹1,000 fine, which would be added to the student’s Pingala portal.
Another question that comes out of this is: even if a student is comfortable with the nature of the test, they may not want to proceed with it and should have the autonomy to opt out , why then are they being charged with a fine? Why is it so necessary for the administration to have everyone undergo the checkup?

The drug screening

[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
Alongside the mandatory health checkup, a separate substance screening has been introduced for the incoming Y26 UG and PG batches. While the current exercise is limited to the freshers, there are potential plans to extend it to other batches in subsequent phases.
Unlike the mandatory pathology check, the drug screening is voluntary. Both UG and PG freshers were informed about the test and its voluntary nature during their orientation. Subsequently, a consent form was circulated through Pingala, through which 944 freshers- 269 UG, 675 PG  initially signed up for the screening.
To ensure that participation was based on informed consent, personal emails were sent to all those who had volunteered earlier, asking them to provide permission again. Following this second round of consent, approximately 200 of the original 944 students withdrew.

In a conversation with Prof. Sudhanshu Shekhar Singh, Head of CMHW, he emphasised that students were asked for their consent again and that any student remains free to withdraw from the process at any point.

Nature of the test

The screening consists of two stages.
The first is the WHO ASSIST – Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test , a World Health Organisation-developed screening tool designed to identify substance-use risks and possible disorders.
The questionnaire uses a standardised scoring system to assess the frequency and extent of substance use. This is followed by a urine test screening for six commonly abused narcotic substances, including cannabis, cocaine and opioids. Only 3 of the 6 substances were identified to us by CMHW.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
Students retain the option to withdraw at multiple stages of the process, either initially through the Pingala consent form, at the Health Centre, or even after completing the questionnaire.
During the first cycle, only 113 students finally reported for the screening. CMHW subsequently transitioned to a dedicated portal and began reaching out to students by phone to reconfirm consent. At the time of reporting, approximately 100 students were yet to confirm or decline participation, while CMHW expects roughly 250 additional students to participate during the ongoing second cycle.

What happens after a positive screening?

According to CMHW Head ,  the screening has been designed to assess health rather than taking disciplinary action.

Each sample is assigned a unique identification number before the test is conducted. According to CMHW, nobody other than the CMHW has access to the information linking these IDs to individual student roll numbers, not even the personnel conducting the test.
Once the screening is complete, the data will be used by CMHW to reach out to students identified as potentially exposed to substance abuse and encourage them to seek counselling and rehabilitation through the already-operating de-addiction centre on campus.
Prof. Shekhar emphasised that CMHW operates exclusively through a wellness and healthcare lens. To ensure sensitive handling of the process, the first round of staff sensitisation has already been completed.

WHAT WAS THE NEED?

The Ministry of  Education in its official letter, makes a broader demographic argument: with 65% of India’s population under the age of 35, students in higher educational institutions form an important part of India’s young population, and their physical and mental wellbeing is necessary to “reap the demographic dividend.”
The Ministry therefore presents health screening as a preventive intervention. Its stated purposes include assessing students’ physical wellness, identifying disease incidence and potential substance abuse, and enabling remediation through medication and counselling. It has asked institutions to conduct initial and annual health check-ups of all students from the 2026 academic year.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
But this raises a more fundamental question: why does improving student healthcare require universal, recurring screening in the first place?
There is an important distinction between providing healthcare and screening an entire population for health problems.
The government’s policy assumes that routinely screening all students will identify problems early enough for intervention to improve outcomes. Whether that assumption holds depends on the conditions/tests being screened for, the prevalence of those conditions, the accuracy and usefulness of the tests, and what happens after a positive result.
The World Health Organization notes that screening is intended to identify apparently healthy people at higher risk so that early intervention can be offered, but cautions that screening programmes require evidence of effectiveness and can also create costs, unnecessary follow-up and harms such as false positives and overdiagnosis.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
Thus the letter by the Ministry of Education, makes its mandate of screening students each academic year clear but does not provide an epidemiological explanation for why an annual health checkup is necessary for the general student population.
There is another question about where the resources devoted to this exercise are being directed. A programme of this scale requires medical personnel, laboratory capacity, data management, follow-up and counselling infrastructure. The government has also called for capacity-building of institutional health personnel and campus-level counselling support. But the screening itself remains the most visible component of the intervention.
This raises a question about whether the priority should be identifying more students with potential health concerns, or strengthening the facilities available to students who already seek help.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > The ‘Not So Annual’ Health Checkup]
At IITK, students who actively seek medical or psychological assistance already interact with the Health Centre (HC) and CMHW. If the objective is to improve student health and wellbeing, resources could alternatively be directed towards strengthening these services from the ground up through measures such as increasing the availability of doctors and counsellors, reducing waiting times, expanding appointment availability, improving specialist and psychiatric support, strengthening follow-up mechanisms, and making treatment and rehabilitation more accessible to students who need them.
*Note : The claim isn’t that these institutions are currently failing at providing support to students, rather a question of whether priority should be given to improving the existing infrastructure and making it more accessible or screening students.

=== [Not So] Little Women ===
Source: https://voxiitk.com/not-so-little-women/
Date: 2026-09-28

For decades, the number of women across IITs remained extremely discouraging. And very few of those who did qualify, made it to the IITs. But it still felt like the classrooms had assumed engineers were men.
In 2016, only 847 women were offered admission to the IITs, a mere 8% of that year’s class. By 2018, that number had nearly doubled. By 2020, it had crossed 20%.

What caused this sudden increase in female admissions in IITs?

It was the introduction of Supernumerary seats.
There is a lot of stigma and confusion around which comes down to a simple lack of awareness of what these seats actually mean, how they’re structured, and why they exist.

[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
What then are supernumerary seats exactly about? Is it just ‘seat stealing’ in the name of gender equality?

Supernumerary seats, as the name suggests, are additional seats created to accommodate women across IITs.
Supernumerary seats were introduced in a phased manner starting in the 2018–19 academic year, requiring each IIT to create new seats so that female representation in every department reached at least 14%. This target rose to 17% in 2019–20, and then to 20% in 2020–21, where it has held steady since.
Scoring well in JEE Main and Advanced alone didn`t ensure female enrollment. The scheme started off of an observation: a significant number of women were qualifying with ranks good enough for admission, yet very few were actually enrolling. In fact, misconceptions and a lack of family support and encouragement kept many from joining in the first place.

[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
How are the seats allocated?

Qualifying women are first allotted to the newly created female-only seats, with top-ranking candidates given priority, before the remaining seats open up to competition between men and women. Since this isn’t reflected in the admissions data, there’s no way to tell whether an individual entered through SSS or not.
Clarity behind its creation:
For the purpose of this article, we interacted with Prof. Timothy A. Gonsalves, former director of IIT Mandi, and the head of JAB sub-committee that introduced the supernumerary scheme.
We also spoke with Prof. Ravinder Kaur, a sociologist at IIT Delhi whose work on gender in STEM offers a grounded look at the barriers women face in higher technical education.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
The core misconception is that these seats “take away” spots that the males would otherwise have rightfully earned. As Prof. Ravinder Kaur found in her research at IIT Delhi, many students had never even heard of the SSS scheme and once it was explained, it was still commonly read as a ‘“girls’ quota,” carrying the assumption that its beneficiaries were somehow less deserving.
In reality, these seats are added on top of the existing pool, giving women a leg up into a better institute or branch without pushing any male student below the rank he’d have secured anyway. In other words, the seats aren’t being taken from anyone. They are created fresh, on top of what already exists.
The scheme also works alongside constitutionally mandated reservations instead of replacing them: women from SC/ST/OBC and other reserved categories continue to benefit under their respective category’s provisions, while SSS separately addresses the gender gap within that same pool. So rather than diluting existing reservation, it layers gender-based support on top of it.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
Beyond that, the scheme aims to push every department, not just the institute as a whole, to 20% female representation, so women aren’t clustered only in traditionally “female-friendly” branches like biotech, while remaining largely absent from CSE, electrical, or mechanical. This directly targets the stereotype that certain branches are simply too demanding or unsuited for women.
Notably, the number of supernumerary seats isn’t fixed, it varies by department and by year, shrinking as a department’s organic female enrollment grows. A department already near 20% needs far fewer additional seats to begin with.

So are we compromising with the quality of education and the overall performance of the male ‘deserving’ students, just to create female engineers?

[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
As far as college academics are concerned, women have historically outperformed their male counterparts; the average CPI data of IIT Kanpur showed that females had an 8% higher CPI in the year 2017-18, and a 7% higher CPI in the year 2018-19 [according to data collected from the Academic Office, IITK]
Since this scheme was introduced in the year 2018, it is clear that the performance of women has no correlation with the ranks at which they were admitted.

Women’s average CPI stayed steady at 8.03 both before and after the scheme (comparing 2016–17 to 2018–19), even as their numbers on campus grew. Men’s average CPI, meanwhile, stayed noticeably lower, at 7.45 in 2016–17 and 7.51 in 2018–19.
So the batch’s overall CPI went up simply because more women, all performing consistently at a higher level, were now part of the batch.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
And it’s not just that women score higher on average, they’re also more consistent. Between 2017 and 2021, the standard deviation of female students’ CPI was consistently lower than that of male students (1.34 vs. 1.62 in 2017-18, narrowing to 1.27 vs. 1.40 by 2020-21). In simpler terms, women’s performance clusters more tightly around the high end, with fewer outliers, fewer academic drop-offs, and a steadier safety margin across the board.

But if women are performing just as well, why can`t they simply make it in, on their own? Why take the unfair advantage of Supernumerary Seats?

“We saw girls sitting beside us in coaching, they got the same resources, then why should they get better options for the same rank in JEE?”
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
Well, what you didn’t see was the isolation most female students experienced. From the highly skewed ratio of women in coaching institutes to the negligible number of female teachers to help them through. Many female students agreed upon the fact that they felt underrepresented and, more often than not, their doubts remained unresolved because the boys crowded the teachers after class. This could also be linked to the lack of faith of the teachers in their female students, and the underlying bias that boys perform better in JEE exams than girls. Their peer groups were intellectually limited because they were forced not to interact with their male batchmates, or they were shoved into ‘girls’ batches’ and not provided the same level of education.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
What you also don’t see is the struggle behind reaching that same batch. For one, they have to go against the social stigma of “If it’s a son, he’ll be an engineer; if it’s a daughter, she’ll be a doctor”. That is to say, if they have equal education or a freedom of choice. And even if they do decide to pursue engineering and crack JEE, most parents don’t agree to sending their girl child to a faraway city for better education, simply because of safety or societal concerns. This affects their preparation and even their choice of college when the time comes.
According to Professor Ravinder Kaur, a study on high-school girls’ aspirations found that girls entering the math/science stream become “less confident” rather than more.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
It is not difficult to picture, therefore, that after being taught to be careful their whole life and losing a part of their confidence in the process, if you ask a girl to give exams like JEE, which assesses her risk-taking skills with negative marking, she wouldn’t be able to express herself completely or freely.
So all these and other factors led to IITs having a female ratio of 8% before the introduction of supernumerary seats.
What is surprising to note is that a lot more than that 8% percentage qualified the exam, so the scale of the drop-off was staggering. In 2016, 4,570 women qualified for JEE, but only 847 took admission, barely one in five. By 2017, the gap had only widened, out of 7,259 qualifying women, just 995 enrolled, per data published by JEE Advanced. Year after year, thousands of women who had already cleared one of the country’s toughest exams simply never made it through the door.

[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
Have supernumerary seats actually had an impact on society, besides just adding more female faces in the IIT campuses?

As Professor Timothy A Gonsalves (the head of the committee that proposed the supernumerary scheme) mentioned in the interview,  “Engineering is done in teams, and diverse teams produce better ideas”.
Real engineering is human-centered and intensely collaborative .
As Prof. Ravinder Kaur (IIT Delhi) points out, Technology isn’t neutral; it carries the culture of the room that designed it. Our world has long been built around a “default male” mindset. This is why Prof. Timothy Gonsalves insisted the scheme was about better engineering, not tokenism.
Diverse teams build better systems not only because they design keeping in mind a world that is half female, which is a reality they reflect, but also because they bring up more ideas and innovative solutions due to the diversity in perspectives and experiences. The different perspectives don`t just add more ideas to the pile, they also ask well rounded questions and identify more problems to work towards. Mixed teams emphasise the importance of aspects such as accessibility, usability, and ethical boundaries alongside raw technical output, not as an afterthought bolted on at the end, but as part of how the problem is defined from the start.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
Back in the 1970s, female students had no choice but to live as paying guests in professors’ homes. The campus was indeed an alienating place for women. With just one or two girls in a batch of 250, they were gazed at like museum exhibits, confined to a tight, isolated bubble, far removed from the fun, chaos, and vibrancy that defined the rest of college life. And change, it’s worth noting, was crawling along at a snail’s pace, if not slower.
Thus, it was surprising when this initiative made decades of difference in just a year. Drawing on 30 years of teaching (from all-male classrooms to today), Prof. Kaur described a marked shift after 2018: more women speaking up in class, less self-isolation, “strength in numbers.”
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
Supernumerary seats didn’t only give them a better college experience and better exposure, but they also increased inclusiveness for them and  gave them a say in the decision-making for students on campus.
More crucially, it altered the mindset of the male batchmates. Many arrive straight out of hyper-competitive, gender-segregated coaching hubs, having rarely interacted with women as intellectual peers. Female students are no longer viewed as alien species who were too scary to be approached for collaborations; they are now seen as equals in projects and competitions who have a lot to add to the table.

What is the way forward?

The female supernumerary seat scheme was initially not intended to be permanent. Today, female representation in IITs is around 20%, but has largely plateaued. At the same time, the gender gap begins well before IIT admission: in JEE (Advanced) 2025, women accounted for 23.2% of registered candidates but only 17.3% of qualifiers. This suggests that reaching 20% IIT enrolment has addressed only part of the underlying disparity. The remaining challenge is to understand and address the factors limiting women’s participation and progression in the domain of engineering.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
Our goal should be to make supernumerary seats unnecessary.
As the policy enters its next phase, its effectiveness could be assessed against broader indicators rather than enrollment at IITs alone. These could include the proportion of female JEE applicants and qualifiers, representation across branches, retention and graduation, academic outcomes, and participation in higher education and employment after graduation.
The existing 20% target could be maintained in the near term while investing in measures that increase female participation throughout the JEE pipeline, but this should be accompanied by regular review to ensure 20% does not become a permanent ceiling. In this framework, 50% could serve as a long-term parity benchmark and aspirational end goal rather than an immediate quota.
[Context: VOX POPULI (VOX), STUDENT MEDIA BODY OF IIT KANPUR - ARTICLES PUBLISHED IN 2026 > [Not So] Little Women]
The scheme could be reviewed every three to five years, with targets adjusted according to the progression data over the years. Rather than setting a fixed end date, an outcome could be considered only when gender representation demonstrates sustained, organic growth across the broader engineering pipeline, rather than at the IIT admission stage alone, indicating that continued intervention is no longer necessary.
Hence, the supernumerary seats can be considered as a major step in evolving the engineering culture in India, creating better engineers with broader perspectives and understanding. Further bridging the gender gap by helping girls find a boat to row against the tide of societal norms and pursue their dreams.
